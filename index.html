The Promise

The Preamble

WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens: JUSTICE, social, economic and political; LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; EQUALITY of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation; in our Constituent Assembly this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do hereby adopt, enact and give to ourselves this Constitution.

The words socialist, secular and integrity were added by the 42nd Amendment (1976). In Kesavananda Bharati (1973), the Supreme Court held the Preamble is part of the Constitution and a key to its interpretation. See full text →

Milestones

From idea to republic

Five moments from a sixteen-year journey — see the full timeline.

1934

The idea is born

M.N. Roy, a Communist leader, becomes the first to propose a Constituent Assembly to frame India's Constitution; the Indian National Congress adopts the demand the next year.

9 December 1946

The Assembly meets

The Constituent Assembly holds its first sitting in New Delhi. 211 members attend; the Muslim League boycotts. Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha is the interim chairman and Dr. Rajendra Prasad is elected President two days later.

29 August 1947

Drafting Committee constituted

A seven-member committee under Dr. B.R. Ambedkar is set up to prepare the draft, building on B.N. Rau's constitutional notes and the Assembly's debates.

26 November 1949

Adoption

The Constitution is adopted and the Preamble enacted. The final calligraphed copies are signed by 284 members. The date is now celebrated as Constitution Day.

26 January 1950

It comes into force

India becomes a republic. The Constitution becomes the supreme law of the land. The date honours the Purna Swaraj declaration of 26 January 1930.

Judgments that made the Constitution

Explore all 20 landmark cases →

Kesavananda Bharati (1973)

13 judges, one doctrine: Parliament can amend the Constitution but cannot destroy its basic structure. The bedrock of Indian constitutionalism.

Maneka Gandhi (1978)

Article 21's 'procedure established by law' must be fair, just and reasonable — unlocking dignity, privacy, livelihood and more as fundamental rights.

Puttaswamy (2017)

A unanimous nine-judge bench holds that privacy is a fundamental right, with a proportionality test for state intrusions.
