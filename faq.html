Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Constitution of India?

It is the supreme law of India — a written document that establishes the framework of government (the Union, the States and local bodies), distributes power among them, guarantees Fundamental Rights and lays down the ideals and duties of citizens. All other laws must conform to it, and any law that violates it can be struck down by the courts.

When was it adopted and when did it come into force?

It was adopted by the Constituent Assembly on 26 November 1949 (now observed as Constitution Day) and came into force on 26 January 1950, when India became a republic. That date was chosen to honour 26 January 1930, when the Indian National Congress first declared Purna Swaraj (complete independence).

How long did it take to frame, and who framed it?

The Constituent Assembly took 2 years, 11 months and 18 days — 11 sessions and 165 days of sittings. It had 299 members after Partition, including 15 women. The seven-member Drafting Committee was chaired by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar. Its philosophical foundation was Nehru's Objectives Resolution (13 December 1946).

Who is called the 'Father of the Indian Constitution'?

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, for his role as chairman of the Drafting Committee and chief architect of the constitutional text. The Constitution's idea, however, was the product of many hands — the Assembly's 299 members, its advisers like B.N. Rau, and draftsmen like S.N. Mukherjee.

How many articles, parts and schedules does it have?

Originally: 395 articles, 22 parts, 8 schedules. With insertions and amendments (through the 106th Amendment), it is commonly cited as about 448 articles, 25 parts and 12 schedules. There have been 106 amendments — the first in 1951, the latest in 2023.

What is the difference between Parts, Articles, Schedules and Amendments?

Articles are the numbered provisions (Article 14, Article 21...). Parts are thematic groups of articles (Part III — Fundamental Rights). Schedules are appendices with lists and details (the Seventh Schedule's three Lists; the Eighth's 22 languages). Amendments are the formal changes made to any of these under Article 368.

What are Fundamental Rights?

Justiciable rights guaranteed in Part III (Articles 12–35), enforceable against the State by moving the Supreme Court under Article 32 or High Courts under Article 226. They fall into six groups: equality (14–18), freedom (19–22), against exploitation (23–24), freedom of religion (25–28), cultural and educational rights (29–30), and constitutional remedies (32). The right to property was removed from Part III by the 44th Amendment (1978) and is now a legal right under Article 300A.

What are Directive Principles of State Policy?

Non-justiciable guidelines in Part IV (Articles 36–51) for governments to follow in making policy — adequate livelihood, equal pay for equal work, village panchayats, a uniform civil code, protection of the environment, and more. They cannot be enforced in court (Article 37), but the Supreme Court has often read them together with Fundamental Rights and used them to interpret statutes and shape policy.

What are Fundamental Duties?

Eleven civic duties of citizens listed in Article 51A of Part IVA, added by the 42nd Amendment (1976) on the Swaran Singh Committee's recommendation (the eleventh — providing education to children aged 6–14 — was added in 2002). They are moral duties, not directly enforceable by courts, but courts may consider them while deciding cases.

What is the basic structure doctrine?

Held in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973): Parliament may amend the Constitution, but it cannot alter or destroy its 'basic structure'. Features identified by courts include the supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic government, secularism, federalism, separation of powers, judicial review, free and fair elections and the rule of law. An amendment that violates the basic structure — as the 39th Amendment's Article 329A and parts of the 42nd were — can be struck down.

How can the Constitution be amended?

Under Article 368 there are three tracks. (1) Simple majority for certain matters outside Article 368 itself — creating new States, citizenship, redrawing constituencies. (2) Special majority — most amendments — in each House separately: a majority of total membership and two-thirds of those present and voting. (3) Special majority plus ratification by at least half the State legislatures for federal provisions — the election of the President, distribution of legislative power, High Courts, etc. And every amendment remains subject to the basic structure doctrine.

What are the five writs the courts can issue?

Under Articles 32 and 226: habeas corpus (produce the detained person), mandamus (command a public authority to perform its duty), prohibition (stop a lower court from exceeding its jurisdiction), certiorari (quash a lower court's or tribunal's order), and quo warranto (question a person's authority to hold a public office).

What is the difference between Article 32 and Article 226?

Article 32 lets you move the Supreme Court for enforcement of fundamental rights — it is itself a fundamental right. Article 226 lets High Courts issue writs for fundamental rights and for any other purpose, so its scope is wider, but it is a constitutional — not a fundamental — right and is discretionary.

What happened to Article 370?

Article 370 gave Jammu and Kashmir special autonomous status when it acceded in 1947. In August 2019, presidential orders read down Article 370 (using Article 367 as amended), removing the special status, and the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 split the State into the Union Territories of Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh. A Constitution Bench upheld the move in December 2023 (In re: Article 370), holding that the provision was a temporary feature.

What is the three-list system?

The Seventh Schedule divides legislative subjects: the Union List (defence, foreign affairs, banking, currency — Parliament alone), the State List (police, public order, agriculture, local government), and the Concurrent List (criminal law, marriage, education, forests — both, with parliamentary law prevailing in case of conflict). Residuary matters go to Parliament (Article 248).

Which language is the Constitution written in?

English in the original — the calligraphed English manuscript and its Hindi counterpart, both signed on 24 November 1949, are the official texts. The 58th Amendment (1987) inserted Article 394A providing for an authoritative Hindi text. The Eighth Schedule lists 22 languages, and Hindi in Devanagari script is the Union's official language (Article 343), with English continuing for official purposes under the Official Languages Act, 1963.

Why is Republic Day on 26 January?

Because at midnight on 26 January 1950 the Constitution came into force, replacing the Government of India Act, 1935 and making India a republic with an elected head of state. The date was deliberately chosen — 26 January 1930 was the day the Congress's Lahore session had declared Purna Swaraj and asked people to observe it as Independence Day.

Terms

Glossary

Key vocabulary for reading the Constitution and its judgments.

Amendment
A formal change to the constitutional text made under Article 368 (special majority, with State ratification for federal provisions).
Basic structure
The core features of the Constitution that even an amendment cannot destroy — from Kesavananda Bharati (1973).
Doctrine of eclipse
Pre-Constitution laws inconsistent with fundamental rights are not dead but dormant; they revive if the inconsistency is removed (Article 13, Bhikaji Narain).
DPSP
Directive Principles of State Policy — non-justiciable policy goals in Part IV.
Federalism
Division of power between the Union and States; India is a federal system with strong unitary features ('quasi-federal').
Judicial review
The power of courts to test laws, executive action and amendments against the Constitution and strike them down if inconsistent.
Money Bill
A Bill dealing only with taxation, borrowing or the Consolidated Fund (Article 110), introduced only in the Lok Sabha; the Speaker certifies it.
Ninth Schedule
A list of laws (starting 1951) protected from fundamental-rights challenge under Article 31B — since 2007 open to the basic structure test for post-1973 laws.
Ordinance
A temporary law issued by the President (Article 123) or Governor (Article 213) when the legislature is not in session; must be laid before it within weeks.
Preamble
The opening statement of the Constitution's aims — 'We, the People of India...' — held to be part of the Constitution and a guide to interpretation.
President's rule
Union takeover of a State's government under Article 356 on failure of constitutional machinery; justiciable since S.R. Bommai (1994).
Repugnancy
Conflict between a Union and State law on a Concurrent List subject; the Union law prevails (Article 254).
Tenth Schedule
The anti-defection law (52nd Amendment, 1985) — disqualification of legislators who defect from their party.
Writ
A formal order of a court — habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto — issued under Articles 32 and 226.
